Citizen science works when ordinary people can collect data that's good enough to be trusted. For roads, the conditions are unusually favorable: the phenomena are everywhere, the observers are already traveling past them, and the sensor to capture them is already in everyone's pocket.

OpenRoad is built to make those observations **structured** — so they add up instead of scattering.

## Who can contribute

- **Drivers** capture signal visibility, signage, and pavement condition on everyday commutes.
- **Cyclists** document bike lanes, hazards, and gaps in the network from the saddle.
- **Pedestrians** flag crosswalks, curb ramps, and sidewalk conditions.
- **Volunteers** run organized surveys of a neighborhood, campus, or corridor.
- **Advocates** build a mapped, open evidence base for safer, better-maintained streets.

No one has to be an engineer. The app collects the sensor data; the review step keeps a human confirming each observation; the export step produces open data anyone can use.

## From data point to signal

Here's the idea that makes crowd-sourced road data powerful:

> One observation is a data point. Many observations of the same location, from different people at different times, become a signal.

A single report of a rough patch or an obstructed signal is easy to miss. But when **repeated observations from multiple users** point to the same spot, that convergence is hard to dismiss. It helps agencies identify locations that may need further review or improvement — and it does so with geolocated, structured evidence rather than anecdote.

<div class="callout">
  <p>OpenRoad doesn't ask volunteers to make engineering judgments. It asks them to record what they see, carefully and consistently, so professionals can prioritize where to look.</p>
