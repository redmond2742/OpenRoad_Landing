Bike infrastructure is where the gap between the official map and the real network is often widest. A city map may show a continuous bike lane; the ride reveals where it vanishes at an intersection, gets blocked by a parked car, or dumps riders into traffic with no transition.

Cyclists are the right people to document this, because they experience it directly — and OpenRoad gives them a way to turn a ride into **structured data**.

## What a ride can capture

Mounted on a handlebar or in a jersey pocket, OpenRoad records the same streams it does in a car:

- **Video** of the lane, its condition, and its obstructions.
- **GPS** to trace the route actually ridden — including detours forced by hazards.
- **Speed and heading** to characterize the ride and the approach to conflict points.
- **Accelerometer** to pick up rough surfaces, expansion joints, and jarring transitions that matter far more on two wheels than four.

## The things inventories miss

Ride data is especially good at surfacing problems that static inventories overlook:

- Bike lanes that end abruptly or lack a safe transition.
- Debris, drainage grates, and pavement seams in the riding line.
- Conflicts at driveways and intersections.
- Gaps between segments that look connected on a map.

Each of these becomes an **observation** you confirm in review, then map and export as open data.

## Building the case

One rider's log is useful. A [group of riders]({{ '/blog/2026/03/24/citizen-science-and-the-road/' | relative_url }}) documenting the same corridors builds something an agency can't easily set aside: repeated, geolocated evidence of where the network breaks down. That's how you turn "the bike lane here is bad" into a mapped, exportable dataset that supports a real conversation about fixing it.
